What Does a Corporate Uniform Program Cost? The 5 Factors That Drive Price

August 8, 2026 · 7 min read · Ordering & Budgeting

Quick answer: There is no single market price for a corporate uniform program — per-garment cost is set by five factors: fabric, construction, order quantity, branding details, and logistics terms. Fabric is usually the largest share; quantity is the strongest lever you control. A manufacturer can give you a firm price within days once the brief is defined — at HARCHOY, MOQ starts at 50 pieces per style, quoted DDP to 50+ countries.

Ask three suppliers "what does a corporate suit program cost?" and you'll get three numbers that differ by a factor of several times. None of them is wrong. They are pricing different programs — different cloth, different construction, different volumes, different trade terms — under the same label.

This guide breaks down the five factors that actually move the price, so you can budget intelligently, compare quotes fairly, and know which levers to pull when the number needs to come down.

Factor 1 — Fabric: The Biggest Cost Driver

Cloth typically represents the largest share of garment cost — commonly in the range of one third to one half — which makes it the single most powerful price lever. The spread is wide:

  • Entry-level polyester-viscose blends keep cost down and handle heavy rotation, but lack the drape and longevity of wool.
  • Mid-weight business wools are the working standard for corporate programs: durable, breathable, and presentable across seasons (our wool fabric guide explains the grades).
  • Premium mill fabrics — fine wools, cashmere blends — move the program into a different price class entirely, appropriate where the attire itself is a brand statement.

Remember that one suit consumes roughly 3 meters of cloth. Every euro per meter is multiplied across every garment in the program — which is why fabric decisions should be made against wearing scenarios, not price lists alone.

Factor 2 — Construction & Design Complexity

Two jackets can look similar on a hanger and cost very differently to make. What drives construction cost:

  • Interlining: fused construction is the economical standard; half-canvas or full-canvas construction adds hand-work and durability.
  • Tailoring details: functional sleeve buttonholes, pick stitching, working cuffs, bespoke linings — each adds operations.
  • Pattern scope: a program covering both men's and women's cuts, multiple garment types (jacket, trouser, skirt, waistcoat), or special-fit sizes multiplies pattern-making and grading work.

The right level of construction depends on who wears the garments and who sees them — the same question that should drive the whole program (why most corporate uniforms fail).

Factor 3 — Order Quantity: Your Strongest Lever

Every order carries fixed costs that exist whether you make 50 garments or 5,000: pattern development, fabric minimums, line setup, and quality control. Quantity decides how those costs spread:

Order sizeEffect on priceTypical use
50 pieces (MOQ)Fixed costs absorbed at entry level — affordable first programPilots, single-department rollouts
100–300 piecesPer-garment price drops as cutting and sewing gain efficiencyCompany-wide programs, first reorders
500+ piecesBest unit economics; stable repeat pricingMulti-location enterprises, annual cycles

Our MOQ guide covers the mechanics in detail. The budget implication: a pilot at 50 pieces validates the program, and the reorder at scale is where the unit economics truly work.

Factor 4 — Branding & Customization Details

The elements that turn clothing into brand identity also add cost — usually modest per unit, but they must be specified early:

  • Woven labels, embroidered logos, contrast stitching or brand-color accents.
  • Custom buttons, zips, and linings carrying brand elements.
  • Individual packaging and delivery kits per location or per employee.

In a brand-first program these details are not add-ons — they are defined during the design direction phase, before sampling, so they are priced accurately from the start (the 10-step process shows where they sit).

Factor 5 — Logistics & Trade Terms

The same garment can land at very different total costs depending on the trade terms in your quote:

  • EXW / FOB: the sticker price looks lower, but freight, import duties, customs clearance and last-mile delivery are still yours to manage and pay — the classic source of budget overruns (the hidden costs of importing).
  • DDP (Delivered Duty Paid): the quoted price is the landed price. HARCHOY delivers DDP to 50+ countries, so the number you approve is the number you pay (how DDP works).

For multi-country programs, also ask how the supplier handles per-location delivery and sizing data collection — fragmentation here is a real, if invisible, cost.

How to Budget a Program (and Compare Quotes Fairly)

  1. Define the brief before asking for prices. Garment types, fabric direction, quantity per style, size range, timeline. Quotes on vague briefs are guesses — and they drift.
  2. Compare like for like. A quote is only comparable when fabric grade, construction level and trade terms match. A cheaper EXW quote can land more expensive than a DDP one.
  3. Sample before bulk. One approved sample is worth more than ten price negotiations.
  4. Budget for total cost of wear, not unit price. A garment that keeps its shape for three years of rotation costs less per wear-year than a cheap one replaced annually. Between cost and quality, the long-run answer is usually quality.

Red Flags: When a Quote Looks Too Good

  • A firm price quoted without any questions about fabric, quantity, or sizing — it will move later.
  • No mention of trade terms or duties in an international quote.
  • No sampling step, or no defined QC standard. Ask what inspection the price includes; at HARCHOY every garment passes a 10-point inspection.
  • Pricing that implies premium fabric at blend-level cost. Somewhere in the chain, the spec is being silently reduced.
