Suit Quality Inspection: The 10-Point QC Checklist We Use on Every Order

August 13, 2026 · 7 min read · Quality & Manufacturing

Quick answer: Every garment we produce passes a 10-point inspection before packing: measurements vs. the spec sheet, fabric integrity, pattern matching, stitching quality, construction shape, collar and shoulders, trims and buttonholes, inside finishing, pressing, and a final overall review. Inspection happens per garment, inside the 15–20 day production window — before anything ships.

When you order 50 suits — or 500 — you will never personally inspect every garment before it ships. That's what quality control is for. And yet most buyers have no idea what their factory's QC actually covers: some factories spot-check a few pieces per batch, some check only visible defects, and many define "inspection" as a final glance before boxing.

The difference matters, because a defect caught at the factory costs minutes; the same defect caught after international delivery costs weeks and a damaged client relationship. This article lays out the exact 10-point checklist we apply to every suit and uniform order — so you know what to demand from any manufacturer, including us. If you're comparing factories, pair this with our 7 questions to ask a bespoke suit manufacturer.

Why a Structured Checklist Beats Spot-Checking

Spot-checking answers one question: "Is this batch mostly fine?" A per-garment checklist answers a better one: "Does every single piece match the approved sample?" Separating inspection into ten distinct categories also stops defects from hiding — a garment can look clean at a glance while its measurements are off by a centimeter. Where the inspection sits in the process matters too: it belongs after production, before packing, as the last gate in the manufacturing journey.

What Each Checkpoint Actually Catches

1. Measurements vs. spec sheet

Every order ships with a graded size spec — the agreed dimensions for each size in the curve. We measure key points of every garment against it. Grading drift is the most common silent defect in bulk orders: size 48 looks right while size 52 has quietly grown. Catching it before packing is the difference between a rework and a return.

2. Fabric integrity

We check each garment for fabric flaws, pulls, and shade variation between panels. Shade differences within one garment are visible in daylight and impossible to fix — which is why fabric is inspected at cutting as well, one reason we hold the fabric supply chain close rather than buying spot lots.

3. Pattern matching & symmetry

Stripes and checks must align at seams, and left and right panels must mirror each other. Pattern matching consumes extra fabric and cutting care, which is why it's often quietly skipped on cheaper production — and why it belongs on your checklist.

4. Stitching quality

Consistent stitch density, no skipped stitches, no puckering. Seam quality is what separates a garment that keeps its shape after months of wear from one that sags at the first pressing.

5. Construction shape

The canvas, interlining, and internal structure determine whether a jacket drapes or just hangs. We check that the chest, lapel roll, and overall silhouette match the approved sample — this is the check that protects the "bespoke" in a bulk order.

6. Collar & shoulders

The collar and shoulder area is the hardest part of a garment to correct after assembly. Any issue here gets caught and fixed before the garment moves to finishing — not discovered by the wearer.

7. Trims & buttonholes

Buttons secured, buttonholes clean, zippers functioning, and branded elements — labels, buttons with logos, custom trims — matching the order spec exactly. For white-label partners, this is also the checkpoint that protects your brand presentation.

8. Lining & inside finishing

The inside of a garment tells you how it was made. We check lining attachment, pocket finishing, and loose threads — the details a buyer's client notices within the first wear.

9. Pressing & finishing

Final pressing sets the presentation: sharp creases where required, no press marks or shine, clean silhouette on the hanger. For corporate programs, this is the state in which the team first sees the uniform.

10. Final review & packing

One last full-garment review against the approved sample, then packing per the order's spec — poly bags, cartons, size sorting, and any per-store or per-employee requirements for uniform programs.

What Happens When a Defect Is Found

The rule is simple: no defective garment leaves the factory. Fixable issues go back to the responsible station — resewing, re-pressing, re-finishing. Structural defects trigger a replacement garment. This rework loop is already budgeted into the standard 15–20 day production window, so inspection does not add days to your order — unlike the alternative of discovering defects after delivery.

How Buyers Can Verify QC Themselves

  • Ask for inspection photos or video before shipment. A reasonable, standard request — and a factory confident in its process will send them without delay.
  • Appoint a third-party inspection service. Common on larger programs; a good factory schedules it as part of the timeline.
  • Look at the production floor itself. Our Factory Live view shows the facility where this checklist is executed — transparency is part of the quality system.
  • Check the sample like a checklist. The approved sample is the contract. If a factory can't tell you how bulk is held to it, keep looking — even at a 50-piece MOQ you deserve the same discipline as a 5,000-piece program.

Common Questions About Suit QC

What is quality control in suit manufacturing?

It's the structured inspection every garment passes before packing. Our 10-point checklist covers measurements, fabric integrity, pattern matching, stitching, construction shape, collar and shoulders, trims, inside finishing, pressing, and a final review against the approved sample.

How many QC checkpoints does a suit order need?

Fewer than five usually misses structural or finishing issues. Ten separate categories ensure a defect in one area can't hide inside another — which is what keeps a bulk order consistent with the sample.

Can buyers inspect garments before shipment?

Yes — inspection photos, a video walkthrough, or a third-party inspection service are all standard requests, and they belong in the production timeline before packing.

What happens when a defect is found during QC?

The garment is pulled for rework or replacement before packing. Fixable issues go back to the relevant station; structural defects mean a replacement garment is produced.

Does quality inspection add to the lead time?

No — the 10-point inspection is built into the standard 15–20 day bulk production window. Inspected garments ship on schedule; post-delivery defect discoveries cost weeks.
